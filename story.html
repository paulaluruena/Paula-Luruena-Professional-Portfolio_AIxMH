Chapter I

The first question

Long before I had the vocabulary for it, I was already an observer of people. Growing up in Madrid, I was fascinated by the invisible mechanics of a room: how a single arrival could shift its temperature, how two friends could hear the identical sentence and walk away with irreconcilable interpretations, how a fleeting glance could quietly rewrite someone’s decision. I wanted to understand what lay beneath the surface of behavior. Why do we react before we reflect? Why do we defend our convictions so fiercely? Why do certain moments embed themselves in memory for decades while others dissolve by evening?

I had no framework for those questions yet, only the compulsion to keep asking them. Twelve years of KUMON mathematics gave me a second instinct that proved just as consequential: the discipline to sit with a problem, alone and unhurried, until it finally yielded. Curiosity and persistence. In retrospect, they were the first two instruments of a researcher.

Living between Spain and the United States sharpened both instincts. Every transition became a natural experiment in perception. The same gesture could read as warmth on one continent and distance on another; the same silence could signal deference or disapproval. I learned early that behavior is unintelligible without context, and that understanding a person begins with understanding the world they are standing in.

Chapter II

A turning point

Some experiences divide a life into a before and an after. Mine arrived when I was a teenager, in the form of a loss that reorganized, almost overnight, my understanding of what matters.

I will not pretend the path forward was graceful. Grief at that age arrives without instructions, and for a long time it posed questions I could not answer. What eventually moved me was not forgetting, but a decision: to let the most difficult chapter of my life become the reason I would spend the rest of it in service of others.

Life is not only about surviving hard moments. It is about allowing each one to make you a better version of yourself.

That decision transformed a childhood fascination into a vocation. Understanding the human mind ceased to be an intellectual pastime and became the most meaningful way I knew to be useful, particularly to people navigating their own hardest moments. It is also why I do not consider empathy a soft skill. For me, it is a form of knowledge, earned the hard way.

Chapter III

Curiosity becomes a discipline

At Tulane, my questions finally found a method. Psychology offered what intuition never could: a rigorous vocabulary for phenomena I had only sensed, and the empirical tools to test them rather than speculate.

Some findings rearranged the way I see everything. Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman demonstrated that human judgment departs from pure logic in ways that are systematic and predictable; Kahneman later distinguished a fast, intuitive mode of thought from a slower, deliberate one. What captivates me is the implication. Even our most seemingly irrational choices follow patterns, and patterns can be studied, anticipated, and designed for. It is the closest thing psychology has to a unifying insight across every field I care about.

In the lab, I learned that insight is built slowly: through standardized protocols, meticulous coding, and data clean enough to deserve trust. At Deep Roots, delivering individualized behavioral interventions, I watched learning theory become tangible, as reinforcement, modeling, and patient repetition helped children acquire skills that once seemed out of reach.

Every other setting deepened the lesson. Observing clinicians at Riverway revealed how disciplined questioning becomes a diagnosis. Supporting callers with Cruz Roja taught me how profoundly a steady voice can alter a moment of crisis. Working alongside Hispanic families at Puentes showed me how language, culture, and circumstance determine what people need. The settings varied. The subject never did: people.

Chapter IV

What I am here to do

If a single thread runs through everything I have done, it is this: I want to help people. Psychology is how I understand them, and understanding is how I help them well.

That is why I do not regard psychology as one career among many. I regard it as a lens that renders every field more human. Wherever people make decisions, seek support, or work together, behavior sits at the center, and the questions a psychologist asks can change what gets built, offered, and led.

My ambition is not modest, and I do not intend it to be. I want to bring rigorous behavioral insight into the places where it can improve the most lives, and to do it with the care that experience has taught me. I am not finished asking my questions. I suspect I never will be. But I know where I want to ask them next.

Chapter V

Where it leads

Psychology and AI in mental health

Of all the directions psychology could take me, AI and mental health is the one closest to my heart. A growing number of people will encounter their first form of support through a screen: an app, a conversational agent, a check-in at two in the morning when no one else is awake. That could expand access to care on a scale the field has never seen, but only if the technology understands people as well as a thoughtful human would.

What draws me to this field is that it needs psychology at every decision point: what a tool should notice, how it should respond, when it should step back and connect someone to a person, and how it earns and keeps trust. These are not engineering questions alone. They are questions about human behavior under vulnerability, and they deserve answers grounded in evidence.

What I want is to explore how people actually behave with these tools: what invites them to open up, what brings them back, and what makes them quietly disengage. That understanding is how we build support that is safe, evidence-based, private, and kind. Observing clinical assessment at Riverway, supporting callers with Cruz Roja, and working with Hispanic families at Puentes taught me what good support feels like. Tulane’s AI & Society Club has taught me what can go wrong. I want to help bring the two together.

Experience has taught me how much it matters when support arrives in time. If technology can help it arrive sooner, for more people, and in their own language, that is work worth dedicating a career to.
